Condition

Menstrual Irregularities & Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

Heavy, prolonged, irregular or unexpected bleeding is common and usually has a treatable cause. Assessment identifies that cause before treatment is chosen.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

Overview

Abnormal uterine bleeding means bleeding that is heavier, longer, more frequent or less predictable than is usual for you, or bleeding at unexpected times.

Any bleeding after the menopause should be assessed promptly by a doctor. Very heavy bleeding with dizziness, fainting or breathlessness needs emergency care.

Common symptoms

  • Heavy periods — flooding, clots, or changing protection every one to two hours
  • Periods lasting longer than seven days
  • Irregular or unpredictable cycles
  • Bleeding between periods or after intercourse
  • Bleeding after the menopause
  • Tiredness or breathlessness from anaemia

Possible causes

  • Endometrial polyps
  • Fibroids
  • Adenomyosis
  • Ovulatory dysfunction, including polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid problems and the years around the menopause
  • Changes in the lining of the uterus (endometrial hyperplasia) and, less commonly, cancer
  • Bleeding disorders and some medicines

Diagnostic approach

After a history and examination, blood tests check for anaemia and relevant hormonal causes. Pelvic ultrasound with 3D imaging assesses the uterus and its lining; saline sonography can outline polyps and fibroids within the cavity. Hysteroscopy and endometrial biopsy are recommended when indicated — for example with persistent bleeding, certain risk factors or postmenopausal bleeding.

Treatment choices

  • Non-hormonal medicines to reduce blood loss
  • Hormonal treatment, including a hormone-releasing intrauterine system
  • Iron replacement for anaemia
  • Hysteroscopic removal of polyps or submucosal fibroids
  • Myomectomy or other procedures for fibroids
  • Hysterectomy, when other treatments are unsuitable or have not helped

Read about hysteroscopic polypectomy, which removes endometrial polyps under direct vision.

When specialist care is appropriate

  • Bleeding after the menopause
  • Bleeding between periods or after intercourse
  • Heavy periods causing anaemia or affecting daily life
  • Bleeding that has not improved with initial treatment
  • A polyp, fibroid or thickened lining seen on ultrasound

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my periods are too heavy?

If bleeding regularly soaks through protection, contains large clots, lasts more than seven days or leaves you exhausted, it is worth having it assessed.

Are endometrial polyps dangerous?

Most are benign. They are usually removed if they cause bleeding, affect fertility or have features that need to be examined in the laboratory.

Will I need a biopsy?

Only when indicated. The recommendation depends on your age, symptoms, risk factors and ultrasound findings.

Can irregular periods be treated without surgery?

Often, yes. Many causes respond to medication once the diagnosis is clear.
