Pelvic Reconstructive Surgery

Reconstructive surgery restores support to the pelvic organs when prolapse is bothersome and non-surgical treatment has not helped enough or is not suitable.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

What it is

Pelvic reconstructive surgery covers a group of operations that correct pelvic organ prolapse.

  • Vaginal repairs of the front or back wall using your own tissue
  • Vaginal suspension procedures for the uterus or vaginal vault
  • Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy — suspension of the vaginal vault
  • Laparoscopic hysteropexy — suspension of the uterus, preserving it
  • Vaginal hysterectomy with repair, when appropriate

Who may benefit

  • Patients with symptomatic prolapse who have tried, or do not wish to use, pessaries and pelvic floor therapy
  • Prolapse recurring after previous surgery
  • Those wishing to discuss uterus-preserving surgery

When it may not be appropriate

  • Mild prolapse without bothersome symptoms
  • When future pregnancy is planned — surgery is usually deferred
  • When medical conditions make surgery higher risk and a pessary is effective

Preparation and shared decision-making

The choice of operation depends on which compartments are affected, previous surgery, sexual activity, your general health and your own priorities. Bladder function is assessed beforehand, as prolapse repair can unmask or change urinary symptoms.

Where mesh is an option — for example in sacrocolpopexy — its potential benefits, specific risks such as exposure or pain, and non-mesh alternatives are explained so that you can make an informed choice.

What to expect

Surgery is under general or regional anaesthetic. Hospital stay is commonly one to two nights. A urinary catheter and vaginal pack may be used for a short time.

Risks

Risks include bleeding, infection, difficulty emptying the bladder, new or persistent urinary leakage, pain during intercourse, injury to bladder or bowel, mesh-related complications where mesh is used, and recurrence of prolapse over time.

Recovery and follow-up

  • Avoid heavy lifting and straining for around six weeks
  • Avoid constipation — fluids, fibre and laxatives if needed
  • Return to desk work is often possible after two to four weeks
  • Pelvic floor exercises are resumed as advised to protect the repair

Frequently asked questions

Will my prolapse come back after surgery?

Recurrence is possible with any repair. The risk varies with the type of prolapse, the operation and individual factors.

Can I keep my uterus?

Uterus-preserving operations are suitable for some patients. This depends on examination findings and your preferences.

Which is better — vaginal or laparoscopic repair?

Neither is best for everyone. Each has advantages for particular types of prolapse and patients.
