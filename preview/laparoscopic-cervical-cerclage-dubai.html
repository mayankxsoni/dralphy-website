Laparoscopic Cervical Cerclage

A laparoscopic cerclage places a supportive stitch high around the cervix. It may be considered for selected patients with cervical insufficiency and previous pregnancy loss.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

What it is

Cervical insufficiency means the cervix shortens and opens too early in pregnancy, without contractions, which can lead to second-trimester loss or very premature birth. A cerclage is a strong stitch placed around the cervix to support it.

A laparoscopic (transabdominal) cerclage is placed by keyhole surgery at the top of the cervix, higher than is possible with a vaginal stitch.

Who may be considered

  • Previous second-trimester loss or very preterm birth despite a vaginal cerclage
  • A cervix that is very short, scarred or partly removed after surgery, making a vaginal stitch impractical
  • Other situations identified after specialist review of your pregnancy history

A referral assessment reviews records of previous pregnancies and procedures, cervical length on ultrasound and other possible causes of pregnancy loss.

When it may not be appropriate

  • When pregnancy loss is more likely to have another cause
  • When a vaginal cerclage or monitoring with progesterone is a suitable first option
  • In a pregnancy that is too advanced for safe laparoscopic placement

Timing: before or during pregnancy

The stitch is most often placed before pregnancy, when the operation is technically simpler and carries no risk to a pregnancy. It can also be placed in early pregnancy, usually before 12–14 weeks, in selected cases. The advantages and risks of each timing are discussed individually.

What to expect

The procedure is done under general anaesthetic through small incisions and usually takes about an hour. Most patients go home the same day or the next morning.

Risks

Risks include those of laparoscopy generally — bleeding, infection and injury to nearby structures — and, when the stitch is placed during pregnancy, a small risk of pregnancy loss. A cerclage improves support of the cervix but cannot guarantee a full-term pregnancy.

Follow-up and delivery planning

  • You will be advised when it is appropriate to try to conceive
  • Pregnancy is monitored with your obstetric team, including cervical length scans
  • The stitch normally stays in place, and delivery is by planned caesarean section
  • The stitch can remain for future pregnancies or be removed at caesarean

Bleeding, fluid leakage, contractions or reduced fetal movements in pregnancy need urgent assessment at a maternity unit.

Frequently asked questions

How is this different from a vaginal cerclage?

It is placed higher on the cervix through the abdomen, and is generally reserved for patients in whom a vaginal stitch has failed or is not possible.

Can I have a vaginal birth with an abdominal cerclage?

No. Delivery is by planned caesarean section because the stitch stays in place.

Does the stitch affect becoming pregnant?

It is not expected to prevent conception. Your individual circumstances will be discussed.
