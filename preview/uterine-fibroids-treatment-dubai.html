Condition

Uterine Fibroids

Fibroids are common, non-cancerous growths of the uterus. Many need no treatment; others cause heavy bleeding, pressure symptoms or affect fertility.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

Overview

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas) are benign growths of the muscle of the uterus. They are very common and vary from a few millimetres to many centimetres. Whether they need treatment depends on symptoms, size, location and your plans for pregnancy.

Common symptoms

  • Heavy or prolonged periods, sometimes leading to anaemia
  • Pelvic pressure, bloating or a feeling of fullness
  • Passing urine frequently, or constipation
  • Pain during intercourse or lower back pain
  • Difficulty conceiving or recurrent pregnancy loss in some cases

Many fibroids cause no symptoms and are found incidentally.

Types and location

  • Submucosal — projecting into the uterine cavity; often linked to heavy bleeding and fertility problems
  • Intramural — within the wall of the uterus
  • Subserosal — on the outer surface; more likely to cause pressure symptoms
  • Pedunculated — attached by a stalk

Diagnostic approach

Pelvic ultrasound, including 3D imaging, shows the number, size and position of fibroids and their relationship to the uterine cavity. MRI may be useful when fibroids are large or numerous, or when procedure planning needs more detail. Blood tests check for anaemia.

Treatment choices

  • Observation, when fibroids are not causing problems
  • Medication to reduce bleeding or, for a limited time, to shrink fibroids
  • Myomectomy — hysteroscopic, laparoscopic or open — to remove fibroids while keeping the uterus
  • Radiofrequency or microwave ablation and uterine artery embolisation, where appropriate and available
  • Hysterectomy, when clinically indicated and when preserving the uterus is not a priority

Each option has benefits, limits and risks, and fibroids can recur after uterus-preserving treatment. Read more about fibroid surgery and myomectomy.

Recovery

Recovery depends on the treatment chosen and, for surgery, on the route of the operation. You will be given advice specific to your procedure, including when to return to normal activity and, where relevant, when it is appropriate to try to conceive.

When specialist care is appropriate

  • Heavy bleeding or anaemia
  • Pressure symptoms affecting the bladder or bowel
  • Fibroids that are growing or are large
  • Fibroids with difficulty conceiving or pregnancy loss
  • A wish to preserve the uterus when hysterectomy has been advised

Frequently asked questions

Do all fibroids need to be removed?

No. Fibroids that are not causing symptoms often need only monitoring.

Can fibroids be treated without removing the uterus?

In many cases, yes. Suitability depends on the number, size and position of the fibroids and on your priorities.

Can I become pregnant after myomectomy?

Many patients do. The advice about timing and mode of delivery depends on the operation performed and will be explained to you.

Are fibroids cancerous?

Fibroids are benign. A cancerous growth resembling a fibroid is rare; any concerning features on imaging will be discussed with you.
