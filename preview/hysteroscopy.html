Treatment

Hysteroscopy

Hysteroscopy looks inside the uterus with a fine camera passed through the cervix. It is used both to diagnose and to treat — including removal of endometrial polyps.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

What it is

A hysteroscope is a thin telescope passed through the vagina and cervix into the uterus. No incisions are needed. Fluid gently opens the cavity so the lining can be inspected and, where needed, treated.

  • Diagnostic hysteroscopy — inspection of the cavity, often with a biopsy
  • Hysteroscopic polypectomy — removal of endometrial polyps
  • Hysteroscopic myomectomy — removal of fibroids within the cavity
  • Treatment of selected intrauterine abnormalities, such as a uterine septum or adhesions

Who may benefit

  • Heavy, irregular or postmenopausal bleeding
  • A polyp, fibroid or thickened lining seen on ultrasound
  • Infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss, in selected cases
  • A displaced intrauterine device

When it may not be appropriate

  • During pregnancy
  • When there is active pelvic infection
  • When ultrasound has already answered the clinical question and treatment would not change

Hysteroscopic polypectomy

Endometrial polyps are overgrowths of the lining of the uterus. Most are benign. Hysteroscopic polypectomy removes them under direct vision, which allows the whole polyp, including its base, to be taken out.

When it is recommended

  • Abnormal bleeding — heavy periods, bleeding between periods or after the menopause
  • Polyps found during investigation of infertility
  • Polyps that are large, multiple or persist on repeat scans
  • Any polyp after the menopause, or with features that should be examined in the laboratory

Small polyps without symptoms in younger patients may sometimes simply be monitored.

Technique

The polyp is seen through the hysteroscope and removed with fine scissors, a tissue-removal device or an electrosurgical loop, depending on its size and position. The procedure usually takes 10–30 minutes and is most often done as a day case.

Pathology assessment

All removed tissue is sent to the laboratory. The result confirms whether the polyp is benign and is reviewed with you at follow-up.

Recovery

  • Mild cramping and light bleeding or discharge for a few days are common
  • Most patients return to normal activities within one to two days
  • Avoid tampons, intercourse and swimming for the period advised

Polyps can recur in some patients.

Preparation

  • The procedure is ideally timed just after a period, if you are still menstruating
  • A pregnancy test may be required
  • Fasting instructions apply if an anaesthetic is planned

What to expect

Depending on the procedure, hysteroscopy is done with local anaesthetic, sedation or a short general anaesthetic. You normally go home the same day.

Risks

Hysteroscopy is generally a low-risk procedure. Uncommon complications include infection, bleeding, perforation of the uterus, fluid overload during longer operative procedures, and adhesions within the cavity afterwards.

Recovery and follow-up

Results of any biopsy or removed tissue are usually available within one to two weeks and are discussed at follow-up, together with any further treatment.

Seek medical attention for heavy bleeding, fever, offensive discharge or increasing pain after the procedure.

Frequently asked questions

Is hysteroscopy painful?

Experiences vary. Cramping is common; the type of anaesthetic is chosen to suit the procedure and your preference.

Are endometrial polyps cancerous?

The great majority are benign. Laboratory examination of the removed tissue confirms this.

Can polyps come back after polypectomy?

Yes, new polyps can form in some patients. Tell your doctor if bleeding symptoms return.

How soon can I try to conceive after polypectomy?

Often from the next cycle, but follow the individual advice you are given.
