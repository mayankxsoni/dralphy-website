Treatment

Minimally Invasive Laparoscopy

Laparoscopy — keyhole surgery — allows many gynaecological operations to be performed through small incisions. It is not the right approach for every patient or every condition.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

What it is

In laparoscopy a thin camera and fine instruments are passed through small incisions in the abdomen, usually 5–10 mm each. The abdomen is gently inflated with gas so the surgeon can see and operate on the pelvic organs on a magnified screen.

Who may benefit

Laparoscopy may be used to diagnose and treat:

  • Endometriosis
  • Fibroids (laparoscopic myomectomy)
  • Ovarian cysts
  • Pelvic adhesions and some causes of pelvic pain
  • Pelvic organ prolapse
  • Conditions requiring hysterectomy
  • Cervical insufficiency (laparoscopic cerclage)

Compared with open surgery, possible benefits include smaller scars, less post-operative pain, a shorter hospital stay and an earlier return to activity. These benefits vary between patients and procedures.

When it may not be appropriate

  • When symptoms can be managed without surgery
  • When a very large mass, extensive previous surgery or certain medical conditions make an open approach safer
  • When there is concern about malignancy that requires a different surgical plan

Occasionally an operation started by laparoscopy needs to be converted to an open procedure for safety.

Preparation

  • A pre-operative assessment, blood tests and review by the anaesthetist
  • Instructions about fasting and regular medicines
  • Bowel preparation only for selected operations
  • A discussion of the planned procedure, alternatives and risks, and written consent

What to expect

Laparoscopy is performed under general anaesthetic. Depending on the operation it may take from under an hour to several hours. Many patients go home the same day or after one night.

Risks

All surgery carries risk. With laparoscopy these include bleeding, infection, blood clots, anaesthetic complications, and — uncommonly — injury to the bowel, bladder, ureters or blood vessels. Your individual risk depends on your health and the complexity of the operation, and will be discussed before you consent.

Recovery and follow-up

  • Shoulder-tip discomfort and bloating from the gas are common for a day or two
  • Light activity is encouraged early; avoid heavy lifting until advised
  • Return to work ranges from a few days to several weeks depending on the procedure
  • A follow-up visit reviews healing and any laboratory results

Seek urgent medical attention for increasing pain, fever, heavy bleeding, persistent vomiting, calf pain or breathlessness.

Frequently asked questions

How many incisions will I have?

Usually three or four small incisions, depending on the operation.

Is laparoscopy always better than open surgery?

Not always. The safest approach depends on the condition and the individual patient.

When can I drive or exercise?

This varies. You will be given advice specific to your operation before you go home.
