Endometriosis Excision / Surgery

Laparoscopic excision removes endometriosis tissue. This page explains the operation itself; for symptoms, diagnosis and non-surgical options, see the endometriosis condition page.

Last updated 16 November 2026

Book a Consultation

What it is

Excision surgery aims to cut out visible endometriosis rather than only burning its surface, while preserving healthy tissue and organ function. It is almost always performed by laparoscopy. For an overview of the condition and medical treatment, see Endometriosis.

Who may benefit

  • Patients whose pain persists despite medical treatment, or who cannot take it
  • Deep endometriosis affecting the bowel, bladder or ureters
  • Selected endometriomas
  • Some patients with endometriosis-related infertility, after discussion with a fertility specialist

When it may not be appropriate

  • When symptoms are well controlled with medication
  • When the expected benefit is small compared with the risk — for example repeated surgery on the ovaries in someone planning pregnancy
  • When pain is mainly driven by other causes that surgery would not address

Alternatives to surgery include pain relief, hormonal treatment, pelvic floor physiotherapy and pain management support.

Preparation and surgical planning

Planning starts with imaging. Specialist ultrasound mapping, with MRI where appropriate, shows where disease is and which organs are involved, so the complexity of surgery can be anticipated.

When the bowel, bladder or ureters are involved, the operation is planned with colorectal and urology colleagues through the multidisciplinary endometriosis team. The goals of surgery, alternatives and risks are agreed with you beforehand.

What to expect

The operation is done under general anaesthetic. Its length and your hospital stay depend on the extent of disease — from a day case for limited disease to several days when bowel or bladder surgery is required.

Risks

In addition to the general risks of laparoscopy, excision of deep disease carries specific risks, including injury to the bowel, bladder or ureters, temporary bladder dysfunction, and reduction in ovarian reserve after surgery on endometriomas. Not every patient gains complete pain relief.

Recovery and follow-up

  • Recovery typically takes two to six weeks, longer after complex surgery
  • Histology results are reviewed at follow-up
  • Medical treatment after surgery may be advised to lower the chance of recurrence
  • Fertility plans are reviewed where relevant

Endometriosis can recur. The likelihood depends on age, disease type and treatment after surgery.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between excision and ablation?

Excision cuts the disease out; ablation destroys the surface with heat. The appropriate technique depends on the type and depth of disease.

Will surgery cure my endometriosis?

Surgery can remove visible disease and often improves symptoms, but it cannot guarantee a cure and recurrence is possible.

Will I need bowel surgery?

Only if imaging and findings show bowel involvement that is causing symptoms. This is planned in advance with a colorectal surgeon whenever possible.
